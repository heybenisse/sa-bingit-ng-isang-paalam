A DOCUMENT THROUGH TIME

Follow its journey.

Select each year to discover the events surrounding the manuscript and the people involved in preserving it.

During his imprisonment at Fort Santiago, José Rizal composed the untitled farewell poem later known as Mi Último Adiós. Its composition is generally associated with December 29, 1896, a day before his execution. Unlike a formally published work, the original manuscript bore no title, signature, or date.

On the afternoon of December 29, Rizal discreetly informed his sister Trinidad that something was hidden inside a small alcohol cooking lamp, commonly called a cocinilla. The folded manuscript had been concealed within it during his imprisonment. This act allowed the document to leave the prison and reach his family.

Sources: Zaide and Zaide (1999); Coates (1968)

After the family's visit, Trinidad and her sisters discovered the tightly folded paper inside the alcohol lamp. According to the historical account cited in the study, they carefully extracted the manuscript using hairpins.

The recovery of the paper was an important moment in its history. What had been concealed from the authorities was now in the hands of Rizal's family. The manuscript survived not through formal publication or institutional protection at first, but through the actions of those who understood its importance.

Source: Coates (1968)

Following the recovery of the manuscript, members of the Rizal family produced handwritten copies of the poem. These copies circulated among Rizal's friends and compatriots. One was sent to Mariano Ponce in Hong Kong, where the poem was first published in 1897.

Because Rizal had left the original manuscript without a title, Mariano Ponce published the poem under the name Mi Último Pensamiento. This early publication helped preserve and circulate Rizal's final written expression beyond the immediate circle of his family.

Sources: Guerrero (1963); Ocampo (2000)

On September 25, 1898, Father Mariano Dacanay published the poem in the newspaper La Independencia under the title Último Adiós.

This publication contributed to the title by which the poem eventually became widely recognized. The change in title is historically significant because it reminds us that the name familiar to later generations was not written by Rizal on the original document. It emerged through the poem's publication and circulation.

Source: Zaide and Zaide (1999)

The manuscript's custody changed after the Rizal family lost possession of the original. One account in the group's paper states that on July 5, 1907, the Bureau of Insular Affairs in Washington received a letter from American author Edwin Wildman offering to sell the manuscript. It further states that the Philippine government acquired the document in 1908 after efforts to examine its authenticity.

However, another passage in the same study, citing Ocampo (2000) and the National Library of the Philippines, states that the original manuscript remained in Trinidad Rizal's custody until the government acquired it in 1935. These dates present a discrepancy in the manuscript's documented provenance and should be checked against the original references and archival records.

Historical note: provenance requires verification

The 1908 and 1935 acquisition accounts should not be treated as one confirmed chronology until the difference is resolved.

Sources cited in the study: Ocampo (2000); National Library of the Philippines (n.d.)

On December 8, 1961, the original manuscript of Mi Último Adiós, together with Rizal's Noli Me Tangere and El Filibusterismo, was stolen from the National Library of the Philippines.

The incident placed these historically significant documents at risk of permanent loss. Their disappearance demonstrated the importance of institutional responsibility and the need to protect original materials that serve as evidence of the nation's past.

Source: National Library of the Philippines account cited in the study

Following negotiations and discreet meetings involving Secretary of Education Alejandro R. Roces and representatives of those responsible for the theft, the manuscripts were recovered in 1962.

The original Mi Último Adiós manuscript was returned to the National Library of the Philippines, where it continued to be preserved as an important historical document. Its recovery marked another significant chapter in its long journey from a concealed handwritten paper to a protected archival object.

Source: National Library of the Philippines account cited in the study

04 / HISTORICAL EXAMINATION

Beyond the document,
the evidence behind it.

A historical document must be examined beyond its appearance and written message. Through external and internal criticism, we investigate the authenticity, credibility, meaning, and limitations of José Rizal's original handwritten manuscript of Mi Último Adiós.

THE HISTORIAN'S APPROACH PRIMARY SOURCE ANALYSIS

How do we examine a historical source?

Historical criticism provides a systematic way of evaluating primary sources. External criticism examines the document itself and the evidence supporting its authenticity, while internal criticism investigates the author's perspective, message, purpose, and limitations.

These approaches allow us to understand not only what the manuscript contains, but also what it can reliably tell us about the past.

Examining the document's physical characteristics, origin, and documented history provides evidence for evaluating its authenticity.

A / 01

Physical Characteristics

The original manuscript is a small, vertically oriented document measuring approximately 9.5 × 15 centimeters. It contains fourteen stanzas written in Spanish across both sides of a single sheet of paper. Its neat and legible handwriting, visible folds, creases, and portions of faded ink distinguish it from later printed reproductions.

These physical characteristics correspond with Ambeth Ocampo's (2019) description of the manuscript, including its dimensions, Rizal's recognizable handwriting, and the marks associated with its concealment.

A / 02

Origin and Historical Provenance

The manuscript's documented history connects it to Rizal's final days at Fort Santiago. On December 29, 1896, Rizal discreetly informed his sister Trinidad that something was hidden inside a small alcohol cooking lamp, or cocinilla. His family later recovered the folded manuscript using hairpins.

Following its recovery, the Rizal family produced handwritten copies that circulated among his friends and compatriots. Mariano Ponce published the poem in Hong Kong in 1897 as Mi Último Pensamiento. Father Mariano Dacanay later published it in La Independencia on September 25, 1898, under the title Último Adiós.

A / 03

Custody and Preservation

The manuscript's history also includes its transfer into government custody, its preservation at the National Library of the Philippines, and its theft on December 8, 1961. It was recovered in 1962 following negotiations involving Secretary of Education Alejandro R. Roces.

Historical accounts cited in the group's research differ regarding when the government acquired the manuscript. One account identifies 1908, while another states that it remained in Trinidad Rizal's custody until 1935. This difference demonstrates the importance of examining provenance through reliable archival records.

HISTORICAL FINDING

Evidence supporting authenticity

The manuscript's physical features, its connection to Rizal's family, the early circulation of handwritten copies, and the documented efforts to authenticate and preserve it provide substantial evidence supporting its authenticity.

Internal criticism examines the content of the manuscript by considering its creator, message, purpose, intended audience, and possible biases.

B / 01

Creator's Background, Role, and Perspective

José Rizal was a physician, scholar, writer, and political thinker whose education and experiences shaped his understanding of Philippine society under Spanish colonial rule. His studies at the Ateneo Municipal de Manila and in Europe exposed him to ideas about society, politics, and the condition of colonized peoples.

As an educated ilustrado and a figure in the reform movement, Rizal emphasized education, civic unity, and peaceful reform. His background helps explain the patriotism and national consciousness expressed in Mi Último Adiós. However, his perspective should not be treated as the exact experience of every Filipino during the period.

B / 02

Main Message, Purpose, and Audience

Mi Último Adiós expresses Rizal's deep love for the Philippines, his willingness to sacrifice for his homeland, and his hope for a future defined by freedom, dignity, and justice.

Beyond being a personal farewell, the poem remembers those who suffered under oppression and expresses hope that future generations would continue striving for the country's freedom. Its message extends beyond Rizal's final moments by presenting patriotism as a commitment to the welfare and future of the nation.

B / 03

Underlying Biases, Motives, and Limitations

The poem reflects the perspective of a condemned man expressing his final beliefs and defending the meaning of his sacrifice. Its circumstances suggest that Rizal intended his message to be discovered and shared. It can therefore be interpreted as an effort to shape how his death would be remembered.

However, the poem presents Rizal's personal perspective rather than a complete account of the political and social conditions of 1896. It expresses emotions and ideals rather than documenting events in detail.

Its use of Spanish also presents a limitation because only a relatively small portion of the population could directly understand the language during the period. Its message therefore reached a wider Filipino audience through subsequent translations.

HISTORICAL FINDING

A personal voice within a national struggle

The poem is valuable for understanding Rizal's convictions, patriotism, and final self-presentation. Nevertheless, it must be interpreted within its historical context and alongside other evidence to avoid treating one person's perspective as the complete experience of an entire nation.

A primary source becomes more meaningful when its claims are examined alongside other historical documents and independent accounts.

C / 01

Family Accounts and Early Publications

Accounts of Rizal's final family visit, the concealment of the manuscript inside the cocinilla, and its recovery by Trinidad and her sisters provide historical context for the surviving document. Its early circulation and publication by Mariano Ponce in 1897 and Father Mariano Dacanay in 1898 further support its connection to Rizal's final days.

C / 02

Trial Records and Personal Correspondence

Rizal's December 1896 military trial records, his courtroom defense, and his correspondence with Ferdinand Blumentritt provide additional context for understanding his position regarding the Philippine Revolution.

These records indicate that Rizal denied leading the Katipunan uprising and maintained that he had advised against the planned revolt when consulted by Pío Valenzuela.

C / 03

Earlier Writings and Political Beliefs

Comparing the poem with Noli Me Tangere, El Filibusterismo, and the principles of La Liga Filipina helps explain the consistency of Rizal's emphasis on education, civic unity, and peaceful reform.

The absence of a direct call to arms in Mi Último Adiós is consistent with the reformist position reflected in these other writings and documented political activities.

C / 04

Language and the Wider Filipino Experience

Historical estimates cited in the study indicate that Spanish was spoken by only a small portion of the population during the 1890s. This linguistic limitation means that the poem initially reflected a message accessible directly to a relatively educated minority.

Therefore, while the poem is a credible record of Rizal's personal convictions, it should be studied alongside other revolutionary and vernacular records to better understand the wider Filipino experience under Spanish colonial rule.

HISTORICAL FINDING

One source, examined through many perspectives

Cross-referencing strengthens the historical interpretation of Mi Último Adiós. It supports the poem's value as evidence of Rizal's personal beliefs while recognizing that broader historical understanding requires additional contemporary records and perspectives.

THE VALUE OF HISTORICAL CRITICISM

To understand the past,
we must learn to question it.

The examination of Mi Último Adiós reveals that historical documents must be understood through both their physical evidence and the meanings they communicate. Its authenticity is supported by its material characteristics and documented history, while its message reveals Rizal's personal convictions, patriotism, and hopes for the Philippines.

Yet, its limitations remind us that no single source can represent the complete experience of an entire nation. Through historical criticism and cross-referencing, the manuscript becomes more than a preserved piece of paper: it becomes a valuable source for understanding the people, ideas, and circumstances that shaped Philippine history.

Jhermi Christian Batallones

Individual Reflection · RPH Project ● Public post

When a familiar story becomes real evidence.

Examining the original handwritten manuscript of José Rizal's Mi Último Adiós gave me a much deeper appreciation of Philippine history by turning a familiar textbook topic into real, physical evidence of a man's final hours. Textbooks usually present the poem simply as a famous farewell written before Rizal's execution on December 30, 1896, often separating its verses from the story of how the document itself survived. Seeing how small the sheet preserved at the National Library of the Philippines actually is. untitled, unsigned, and folded tightly enough to be hidden inside an alcohol lamp (cocinilla) showed me that history is shaped not only by major political events, but also by quiet, deliberate acts of preservation under pressure. Learning how Trinidad Rizal and her sisters carefully retrieved the fragile paper using hairpins and transcribed copies for his compatriots also made me recognize the vital, often-overlooked role of his family in keeping his final message alive.

At the same time, applying external and internal criticism taught me that even a celebrated primary source has to be read carefully rather than treated as the entire narrative of 1896. Cross-referencing the poem with Rizal's ilustrado background, his December 1896 military trial, and the fact that only a small minority of Filipinos spoke Spanish made me realize that Mi Último Adiós reflects Rizal's personal perspective and moral convictions, rather than the direct voices of ordinary Filipinos or the revolutionaries fighting on the ground. Traditional textbook summaries rarely highlight the tension between his push for peaceful reform and the armed revolution waged partly in his name, or how he used his final poem to define his execution as a willing sacrifice rather than a rebel's punishment. Ultimately, working on this study taught me that primary sources do not replace broader historical evidence. Instead, they humanize historical figures while challenging us to look closely at the motives, context, and limitations behind every surviving document.

A farewell that lives beyond its words.

The study of the original manuscript of Jose Rizal’s Mi Ultimo Adios has shown me that history is not just a list of dates, events, and names written in textbooks. Sometimes it's in the smallest details: the faded ink of a printed document, the face of a man nearing the end, and the memories of a timeworn object. Before this analysis, I believed that the document was Rizal’s final farewell to his country. Today I see it as a more personal revelation, a glimpse of the soul of a man whose patriotism endured even as he was expected to leave.

The manuscript has a story of its own, aside from what is written on it. Folded and tucked inside an alcohol burner, it could have easily been lost to time. But, because of the efforts of those who recognized its value, it was reclaimed and preserved for generations to come. That made me realize history doesn’t live by itself. And it is sustained by people who know that even the smallest scraps of the past are worth remembering. What appears to be nothing more than a piece of paper can carry the memories and ideals of an entire nation.

Reading this primary source, rather than the short summaries typically found in textbooks, enabled me to go beyond Rizal’s execution and the poem’s well-known reputation. It brought me closer to his patriotism and made me realize that every historical figure is a human being with thoughts, feelings, and beliefs. Simultaneously, I discovered that a single document cannot convey the entire narrative. The poem is a reflection of Rizal’s own ideals, but it cannot fully embody the experience of a whole nation living under Spanish colonial rule. Therefore, to understand history, we must look beyond the written word, question the evidence, and acknowledge the stories that are not told.

Finally, this study changed my perception of the relationship between the past and present. I have come to understand that a farewell is not always an ending. Sometimes it becomes the start of something that lasts long after its author is gone. Rizal's handwriting is from another century, but its ideals still resonate with readers today. The wonderful thing about keeping history alive is that even if the ink fades and the pages become old, their meaning can live on.

Patriotism is remembered through action.

Mi Último Adiós taught me how deeply José Rizal's love for the Philippines was ingrained in his heart. Even in the final moments of his life, he never lost hope that one day his beloved country would attain the freedom it deserved. For Rizal, death was not something to fear, especially if it meant giving his life for the freedom and future of his people. His sacrifice was not only for himself but for the generations of Filipinos who would come after him.

What struck me the most was how Rizal accepted his fate with dignity and purpose. He knew that he would never witness the freedom he had long dreamed of, yet he still believed that his sacrifice would mean something to his country. Through his pen and his literature, he fought for a nation that he wanted to see free from oppression and injustice. It made me realize that fighting for one's country does not always mean holding a weapon, but also having the courage to stand for what is right, even when it comes with sacrifices.

The poem also reminded me of the countless Filipinos who offered their lives without ever seeing the freedom they fought for. Some died without recognition, while others left behind families who continued to grieve for them. Rizal's farewell was not only about his own death but also about remembering those who suffered for the same land and the same dream. Their sacrifices should never be forgotten because the freedom we experience today was once something they could only hope for.

As a Filipino, Mi Último Adiós made me appreciate the country and the freedom that we have today. We may no longer face the same struggles as Rizal did, but it does not mean that our responsibility to our country has ended. We can continue what he fought for by standing against injustice, speaking the truth, and doing what we can for the welfare of our fellow Filipinos.

I believe that Rizal's greatest message is that loving one's country goes beyond words. It is reflected in our actions, sacrifices, and willingness to protect the dignity and freedom of our people. Rizal may have offered his last breath to the Philippines, but his hope for the country continues to live through every Filipino who chooses to stand for what is right. Freedom was once a dream that Rizal was willing to die for, and now that we have it, it is our responsibility to make sure that his sacrifice was never in vain.

Understanding the man behind the manuscript.

Analyzing Mi Último Adiós alongside Rizal’s December 1896 trial records made me feel a deep sense of admiration for his quiet courage, while giving me a much clearer understanding of his final days. School lessons usually jump straight from his arrest to his execution at Bagumbayan, treating the poem purely as a sad goodbye. Reading the verses next to the military court's guilty verdict moved me because it showed a condemned man facing death without bitterness or anger, actively answering rebellion charges on his own terms. Leaving the manuscript untitled and unsigned while hiding it for his family to share shows the calm determination he had in shaping how his death would be remembered, as a willing sacrifice rather than a traitor's punishment.

At the same time, this study made me feel a bittersweet awareness of the distance between Rizal and the ordinary Filipinos fighting for freedom, helping me better understand the limits of historical sources. Because Rizal wrote in Spanish, only a small, educated minority could read his words right away, while the masses battling in the fields had to wait for Tagalog translations to feel the weight of his farewell. Recognizing that language barrier made me empathize with both Rizal’s lonely, peaceful pursuit of reform inside his cell and the urgent, armed struggle of the revolutionaries outside. Ultimately, I realized that a primary source carries real human emotion alongside its limits, and testing it against court documents and historical context is what brings the true depth of Philippine history into focus.

Individual Reflection · RPH Project ● Public post

Beyond the ink and paper.

From what I have observed, what strikes me most is not just the preservation of centuries-old ink and paper, but the true intention behind the man who wrote them. Contrary to the common misconception that Rizal’s writings were a direct call to violent uprising, viewing these preserved works reminds me that his letters, novels, and final farewell were fundamentally declarations of reformation rather than revolution. He was able to recognize and weaponize years of colonial abuses through compositions and his unyielding love for the Philippines in his farewell poem. Rizal sought to awaken the Filipino consciousness to reform minds, expose injustice, and demand dignity through reason and moral courage. Yet, in a profound historical irony, it was precisely this peaceful plea for enlightenment and reform that ignited the fire within the oppressed Filipinos, once their eyes were opened to their own worth and suffering, no colonial force could contain the revolutionary spirit that followed and the heat that surged from continuous maltreatment and imperialist dictatorship of the Spanish Colonial Ruling.

Seeing the historical figure as a person.

Studying the original manuscript of Mi Último Adiós made the poem feel more real to us. We have encountered Rizal and his works many times, so it was easy to think of him mainly as a national hero rather than as a person. After studying Mi Último Adiós, we understood Rizal's farewell not only as an expression of patriotism, but also as a deeply personal final message that continues to reach Filipinos even today.
